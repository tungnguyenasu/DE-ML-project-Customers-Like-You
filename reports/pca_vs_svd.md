# PCA vs Truncated SVD (ML5)

Generated by `analysis.ipynb` (section 5). Both methods were fitted on the training matrix with the
purchase value and k chosen on the validation split (1/0 bought, 5,456 customers x
3,680 products, k = 70), with the same ARPACK solver and seed 42. The decision being
tested was written before fitting: [method_decision.md](../method_decision.md). A first run at
k = 50 on the log(1 + units) matrix, before k and the purchase value were chosen, gave the same
conclusions.

## Comparison table

| Measure | PCA | Truncated SVD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matrix and k used | 1/0 bought, k = 70 | 1/0 bought, k = 70 |
| Fit time (seconds) | 0.27 | 0.29 |
| Peak memory during fit (MB) | 10.8 with implicit centring; 493 if centred explicitly | 15.6 |
| Size of stored factors (MB) | 5.15 (factors + 3,680 product averages) | 5.12 |
| Variance explained or relative reconstruction error | 31.3% of variance explained; relative error 0.809 | Relative error 0.809 (keeps 34.5% of the matrix) |
| Hit rate at 10 on held-out purchases | 40.6% rebuilt row; 24.9% cosine | 40.4% rebuilt row; 24.5% cosine |
| Best-seller baseline hit rate at 10 | 26.9% | 26.9% |
| What component 1 represents, in words | Also overall buying, measured from the average customer: 100.0% of products weigh positively (correlation with buyers 0.95). The first contrast between kinds of shops is component 2: home decoration against children's lunch and party items. | Overall buying: every product weighs positively, more for popular products (correlation with buyers 0.99); a customer's score follows how much they buy (correlation 0.98). |
| Top 10 for all 5,456 customers (seconds) | 0.29 | 0.31 |

Fit time is the best of 3 runs. Peak memory is what the fit allocates (`tracemalloc`), not counting
the input matrix. Hit rate at 10 is the share of the 1,903 test customers with at
least one held-out purchase in their top 10. "Cosine" is the brief's ML3 rule; "rebuilt row" scores
with the customer's row of A_k (dot products instead of cosines), the rule chosen on validation.

## What our numbers show

1. **On this matrix, PCA and SVD give practically the same model.** The average customer that PCA
   removes is only 4.7% of the matrix: centring shifts every zero by
   0.020 on average. The reconstruction errors agree to three decimals
   (0.809 vs 0.809). The first five components
   point the same way (cosine at least 0.962), and the two 70-dimensional subspaces
   agree to 0.968. The "also bought" lists share 96% of their products.
2. **The headline numbers differ only because they divide by different totals.** "Keeps
   34.5% of the matrix" (SVD) and "explains 31.3% of the
   variance" (PCA) describe the same fit. PCA's total leaves out the average customer.
3. **Centring is costly only if it is done by hand.** scikit-learn's implicit centring keeps PCA as
   cheap as SVD (0.27 s against 0.29 s). Centring the
   matrix explicitly makes all 20,078,080 cells non-zero and pushes peak memory to 493 MB.
4. **Component 1 is overall buying in both methods.** Removing the average does not remove the
   differences in how much customers buy, and those are the biggest variation. The first contrast
   between kinds of shops (home decoration against children's lunch and party items) is component 2
   in both.
5. **Centring matters for the scoring rule, not the method.** The brief's cosine rule normalises each
   product vector to length 1, which throws away how much a product sells. That leaves both methods
   below the baseline (24.5% SVD, 24.9% PCA, against
   26.9%). The rebuilt row keeps that signal: 40.4% (SVD) and
   40.6% (PCA). Without its average, PCA's rebuilt row drops to
   39.2%: the average customer is worth
   1.4 points.
6. **Decision: Truncated SVD stays.** By the rule written in ML1,
   PCA's largest lead is +0.3 points, under the 1-point bar. SVD
   works on the sparse matrix as it is, needs no averages stored, and its error is the "what we lose"
   number for the memo.
